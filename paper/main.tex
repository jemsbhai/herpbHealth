\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has become a standard architecture for
grounding large language model (LLM) responses in domain-specific knowledge,
and healthcare represents one of its most promising application domains.
Clinical decision support systems, drug interaction checkers, and medical
question-answering services all benefit from retrieving relevant medical
literature or patient records before generating a response. However, deploying
RAG in healthcare settings raises serious privacy concerns: patient queries may
reveal protected health information (PHI), and medical document corpora may
contain sensitive clinical data. Existing privacy-preserving approaches based
on homomorphic encryption or differential privacy either introduce substantial
computational overhead or degrade retrieval quality through noise injection.

We present a two-server architecture for privacy-preserving healthcare RAG
that achieves \emph{exact} retrieval over encrypted medical embeddings with
information-theoretic security guarantees. Our protocol uses additive secret
sharing over the native 64-bit integer ring $\Z_{2^{64}}$ to compute cosine
similarity without revealing either the patient query or the document
embeddings to any single server. Because the computation is exact (no
approximation, no noise), the retrieved documents are identical to those
a plaintext system would return, preserving downstream LLM answer quality
perfectly. This property is not an empirical observation but a mathematical
consequence of exact integer arithmetic: noise-based methods (CKKS, DP)
cannot provide this guarantee.

We evaluate our system on three medical question-answering benchmarks
(PubMedQA, MedQA, MedMCQA) using clinical embedding models, demonstrating
99.7\% Recall@5 agreement with plaintext retrieval, sub-millisecond
per-query latency, and identical end-to-end answer quality as measured by
accuracy on medical QA classification tasks. Differential privacy baselines
degrade clinical accuracy by 52 to 58\% through retrieval rank perturbation.
Our approach provides over 9{,}000$\times$ speedup over CKKS-based
homomorphic encryption while requiring 54$\times$ less per-document storage,
with security guarantees that map directly to HIPAA technical safeguard
requirements.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% ============================================================

Large language models have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in medical
reasoning, clinical summarization, and biomedical question answering
\cite{singhal2023medpalm, nori2023gpt4medicine}. Retrieval-augmented
generation extends these capabilities by grounding LLM responses in
authoritative medical sources, reducing hallucination and enabling
evidence-based clinical decision support
\cite{xiong2024benchmarking_medical_rag}. RAG architectures have been applied
to drug interaction checking \cite{zakka2024almanac}, clinical trial matching
\cite{jin2024trialmatch}, radiology report generation
\cite{jeong2024xrem}, and general medical question answering
\cite{xiong2024benchmarking_medical_rag}.

However, healthcare RAG raises serious privacy concerns: both the query
and the document corpus may contain protected health information (PHI)
subject to HIPAA \cite{hipaa1996} and the GDPR \cite{gdpr2016}. A
clinician's query may reveal patient diagnosis or treatment history, and
the document corpus may contain sensitive clinical notes.

Existing approaches each have significant limitations.
\emph{Homomorphic encryption} (e.g., CKKS \cite{cheon2017ckks}) allows
computation on encrypted data but introduces approximation noise and
operates orders of magnitude slower with megabyte-scale ciphertexts
\cite{zuber2021knn}.
\emph{Differential privacy} adds calibrated noise to embeddings, but
the noise directly degrades retrieval quality to clinically unacceptable
levels \cite{lyu2020dp}.
\emph{Trusted execution environments} (Intel SGX, ARM TrustZone) rely on
hardware trust assumptions repeatedly violated by side-channel attacks
\cite{nilsson2020sgx}.

We propose a fundamentally different approach: two-server additive secret
sharing over the native 64-bit integer ring $\Z_{2^{64}}$. Our protocol
splits each embedding vector into two random shares, distributed to two
non-colluding servers. Neither server learns anything about the original
embedding from its share alone (information-theoretic security), yet the
servers can collaboratively compute exact inner products using a pre-computed
matrix-vector Beaver triple \cite{beaver1991triples}. Because the arithmetic
is exact (integer addition and multiplication with no approximation), the
similarity scores recovered by the client are bit-for-bit identical to
plaintext computation, guaranteeing that the retrieved documents and
downstream LLM answers are unchanged.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Scope.}
The cryptographic foundations (security proofs, Beaver triple construction)
are presented in a companion paper \cite{syed2026rpb}. This paper
provides the first end-to-end integration within a clinical RAG architecture,
addressing HIPAA compliance, LLM generation boundaries, and evaluation on
medical benchmarks.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
(1)~The first privacy-preserving RAG system evaluated on clinical benchmarks
with provably exact retrieval.
(2)~Evaluation on three medical QA datasets (PubMedQA, MedQA, MedMCQA)
with multiple embedding models.
(3)~A concrete mapping of information-theoretic security to HIPAA technical
safeguards, including the Minimum Necessary standard.
(4)~Over 9{,}000$\times$ speedup and 54$\times$ storage reduction versus
CKKS, with zero quality degradation.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented Generation in Healthcare}
\label{sec:bg_rag}

RAG architectures \cite{lewis2020rag} augment language model generation by
prepending retrieved context passages to the input prompt, addressing
hallucination and enabling access to institution-specific clinical knowledge.
Recent work has applied RAG to clinical decision support and drug
information retrieval \cite{zakka2024almanac}, and to medical question
answering
\cite{xiong2024benchmarking_medical_rag}. In each case, retrieval quality
directly determines response quality.
The retrieval step computes cosine similarity between a query embedding
$\query \in \R^d$ and each document embedding $\doc_i \in \R^d$,
returning the top-$k$ most similar documents to the LLM.


\subsection{Privacy Requirements for Clinical Data}
\label{sec:bg_privacy}

Healthcare data is subject to HIPAA \cite{hipaa1996} in the US and the
GDPR \cite{gdpr2016} in Europe. For RAG systems, privacy concerns are
twofold: the \emph{query} may reveal PHI (e.g., a clinician describing a
patient's symptoms), and the \emph{document corpus} may contain sensitive
clinical notes. Even de-identified text is vulnerable to re-identification
attacks \cite{elemam2011reidentification}.


\subsection{Privacy-Preserving Computation Approaches}
\label{sec:bg_ppml}

\textbf{Homomorphic encryption.}
The CKKS scheme \cite{cheon2017ckks} supports approximate arithmetic on
encrypted data and has been applied to similarity search,
but introduces approximation noise and
requires megabyte-scale ciphertexts with hundreds of milliseconds per query
\cite{zuber2021knn}.
\textbf{Differential privacy} \cite{dwork2006dp} adds calibrated noise to
query or document embeddings
\cite{lyu2020dp}, but the privacy-utility tradeoff renders
retrieval unusable at meaningful privacy levels.
\textbf{Secure multi-party computation} (MPC)
\cite{yao1986garbled, goldreich1987mpc} enables joint computation
over private inputs. Protocols based on secret sharing
\cite{shamir1979sharing, ben-or1988completeness} with Beaver triples
\cite{beaver1991triples} provide information-theoretic security.
MPC has been explored for privacy-preserving ML
\cite{mohassel2017secureml}, but applications to healthcare RAG remain
largely unexplored.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat_model}

We consider the standard honest-but-curious (semi-honest) two-server model.
A \emph{client} (the clinician) performs private similarity search with the
help of two non-colluding \emph{servers} deployed under separate
administrative domains. All channels use TLS; the client is trusted.
The security guarantee is \emph{information-theoretic}: neither server
learns any information about the query or document embeddings, regardless
of computational power. We consider two variants:
\textbf{query-private mode}, where only the query is protected (public
knowledge base); and \textbf{mutual-privacy mode}, where both query and
corpus are secret-shared (sensitive clinical records).


\subsection{Two-Server Deployment for Healthcare}
\label{sec:deployment}

The two-server model maps naturally to existing healthcare IT infrastructure.
\Cref{fig:architecture} illustrates the Hospital~+~Cloud configuration,
where Server~1 runs on hospital premises and Server~2 on a BAA-covered
cloud provider; non-collusion is enforced by organizational separation.
Alternative configurations include Cloud~+~Cloud (two providers under
separate BAAs) and federated health systems (Server~1 at Hospital~A,
Server~2 at Hospital~B) for cross-institutional private retrieval.
In all cases, the client initiates queries, reconstructs results, and
passes retrieved documents to a locally hosted LLM that never
communicates with either server (see \Cref{sec:pipeline}).

\subsection{Protocol Overview}
\label{sec:protocol}

We summarize the two-server protocol for private cosine similarity search.
Full details and security proofs are provided in \cite{syed2026rpb}.

\subsubsection{Setup (Offline Phase)}
Let the document corpus consist of $N$ pre-normalized embedding vectors
$\doc_1, \dots, \doc_N \in \R^d$. Each vector is encoded to a fixed-point
integer representation using a scale factor $2^k$:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\doc}_i[j] = \lfloor \doc_i[j] \cdot 2^k \rceil \in \Z_{2^{64}}
    \label{eq:encoding}
\end{equation}
where $\lfloor \cdot \rceil$ denotes rounding to the nearest integer. For
$d = 384$ and $k = 16$, the maximum inner product magnitude is bounded by
$d \cdot (2^k)^2 = 384 \cdot 2^{32} \approx 2^{41}$, well within the
$2^{63}$ positive range of signed interpretation in $\Z_{2^{64}}$.

\textbf{Query-private mode:} The encoded document matrix
$\hat{D} \in \Z_{2^{64}}^{N \times d}$ is stored in plaintext on both
servers.

\textbf{Mutual-privacy mode:} The client generates additive shares of each
document vector:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\doc}_i = \hat{\doc}_i^{(1)} + \hat{\doc}_i^{(2)} \pmod{2^{64}}
    \label{eq:doc_sharing}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\doc}_i^{(1)} \xleftarrow{\$} \Z_{2^{64}}^d$ is uniformly random
and $\hat{\doc}_i^{(2)} = \hat{\doc}_i - \hat{\doc}_i^{(1)}$. Server~1
receives $\{\hat{\doc}_i^{(1)}\}$, Server~2 receives $\{\hat{\doc}_i^{(2)}\}$.
Additionally, a matrix-vector Beaver triple $(A, B, C)$ is pre-computed and
distributed, where $A \in \Z_{2^{64}}^{N \times d}$,
$B \in \Z_{2^{64}}^{d}$, $C = A \cdot B \in \Z_{2^{64}}^{N}$, and
$C^{(1)} + C^{(2)} = C$ \cite{beaver1991triples}.
Because the document corpus is static, each server also precomputes its
share of the document mask $E^{(j)} = \hat{D}^{(j)} - A^{(j)}$ and
opens $E = \hat{D} - A$ by exchanging shares with the other server
during setup. This is a one-time cost, amortized across all future queries.

\subsubsection{Query (Online Phase)}
The client encodes and shares a query vector $\query \in \R^d$:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\query} = \hat{\query}^{(1)} + \hat{\query}^{(2)} \pmod{2^{64}}
    \label{eq:query_sharing}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Query-private mode:} Each server computes its local share of the
matrix-vector product $\hat{D} \cdot \hat{\query}$ using its query share
and the plaintext documents. The client reconstructs the similarity scores
by summing the shares.

\textbf{Mutual-privacy mode:} The document mask $E = \hat{D} - A$ was
precomputed and opened during setup. At query time, each server computes
and broadcasts only its share of the query mask
$F^{(j)} = \hat{\query}^{(j)} - B^{(j)}$, then uses the public values
$E$ and $F = F^{(1)} + F^{(2)}$ together with its private triple share to
compute its share of the inner product. The client reconstructs
the exact inner products from the two shares. Because $E$ is precomputed,
the total online inter-server communication is $O(d)$ field elements
(a single masked query vector), independent of both corpus size and
the number of queries.

\subsubsection{Result Recovery}
The client receives integer similarity scores
$s_i = \hat{\query} \cdot \hat{\doc}_i$ for each document. These are
proportional to the true cosine similarities with a known public scale
factor $2^{2k}$:
\begin{equation}
    \text{sim}(\query, \doc_i) \approx \frac{s_i}{2^{2k}}
    \label{eq:recovery}
\end{equation}
The client selects the top-$k$ documents and passes them to the LLM for
response generation. Because the integer arithmetic is exact (no noise, no
approximation beyond the initial fixed-point encoding), the ranking is
identical to plaintext retrieval for all practical encoding precisions.


\subsection{End-to-End RAG Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

Our complete healthcare RAG pipeline consists of five stages:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Embedding:} The client encodes the clinician's query using a
    medical embedding model (e.g., MedCPT, MiniLM) to produce
    $\query \in \R^d$.
\item \textbf{Encoding and sharing:} The client encodes $\query$ to
    fixed-point integers and generates additive shares.
\item \textbf{Private retrieval:} The two servers execute the secret-sharing
    protocol to compute similarity scores without learning the query or
    documents.
\item \textbf{Reconstruction and ranking:} The client reconstructs scores,
    selects the top-$k$ documents, and retrieves their plaintext content
    from a local secure store (e.g., an on-premises FHIR server or
    document repository).
\item \textbf{Secure generation:} The retrieved documents and query are
    submitted to an LLM within the client's trust boundary (locally hosted
    open-weight models such as Llama~3 or Meditron \cite{chen2023meditron},
    or a BAA-covered endpoint). Sending PHI to a public LLM API would
    negate the protocol's privacy guarantees.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Privacy Analysis}
\label{sec:privacy}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Information-Theoretic Security}
\label{sec:it_security}

The security of additive secret sharing over $\Z_{2^{64}}$ is
information-theoretic: each share is uniformly random and statistically
independent of the secret. Formally, for any secret $x \in \Z_{2^{64}}$
and share $x^{(1)} \xleftarrow{\$} \Z_{2^{64}}$, the conditional distribution
of $x^{(1)}$ given $x$ is uniform over $\Z_{2^{64}}$. A single share reveals
zero bits of information about the secret, regardless of the adversary's
computational power.

This extends to vectors: each share $\hat{\doc}_i^{(j)}$ is uniformly
random in $\Z_{2^{64}}^d$, and the Beaver triple masks ($E$, $F$) revealed
during the protocol are uniform and independent of $\hat{D}$ and
$\hat{\query}$.

\begin{theorem}[Informal; see \cite{syed2026rpb}]
In the honest-but-curious model with non-colluding servers, the two-server
additive secret sharing protocol reveals no information about the query
embedding $\hat{\query}$ or the document embeddings $\{\hat{\doc}_i\}$ to
either server, except what is implied by the final output (the top-$k$
document indices) delivered to the client.
\end{theorem}


\subsection{Mapping to HIPAA Technical Safeguards}
\label{sec:hipaa_mapping}

We map our protocol to the HIPAA Security Rule (45 CFR \S 164.312):
\emph{Access Control} (\S 164.312(a)): each server accesses only its share;
no single server can reconstruct PHI.
\emph{Audit Controls} (\S 164.312(b)): servers log share-level operations
that reveal no PHI content.
\emph{Integrity} (\S 164.312(c)): exact integer arithmetic ensures no
approximation-induced corruption; integrity against malicious servers
requires MAC-based verification \cite{damgard2012spdz}, left to future work.
\emph{Transmission Security} (\S 164.312(e)): TLS provides encryption in
transit; shares add defense-in-depth.
\emph{Minimum Necessary} (\S 164.502(b)): the PHI disclosed to each server
is exactly zero bits, the strongest possible realization of this standard.
Compared to HIPAA Safe Harbor de-identification, our approach provides
strictly stronger protection: re-identification attacks
\cite{elemam2011reidentification} are impossible because there is no
data to re-identify.


\subsection{Enforceability of the Non-Collusion Assumption}
\label{sec:non_collusion}

Under HIPAA, any entity processing PHI must sign a Business Associate
Agreement (BAA), with civil penalties up to \$1.9M per violation category
and criminal penalties up to \$250K and imprisonment \cite{hipaa1996}.
When servers operate under separate BAAs, collusion requires both
organizations to jointly violate independent legal obligations---qualitatively
different from computational hardness assumptions that may be silently
broken. This information-theoretic guarantee also provides an advantage over
lattice-based HE schemes whose security assumptions may be invalidated by
future algorithmic advances; our protocol's security holds against
quantum computers, with non-collusion enforced through legal mechanisms.

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiments}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}

\subsubsection{Datasets}
We evaluate on three medical QA benchmarks:
PubMedQA \cite{jin2019pubmedqa} (1{,}000 biomedical yes/no/maybe questions),
MedQA \cite{jin2021medqa} (1{,}000 USMLE-style multiple-choice questions),
and MedMCQA \cite{pal2022medmcqa} (2{,}000 questions from Indian medical
examinations spanning 21 subjects). Retrieval corpora range from 1{,}000 to
2{,}000 documents.

\subsubsection{Embedding Models}
We use MedCPT \cite{jin2023medcpt} (768-d, trained on PubMed search logs)
and all-MiniLM-L6-v2 \cite{reimers2019sentencebert} (384-d, general-purpose baseline).

\subsubsection{Baselines}
We compare against plaintext retrieval (float32 cosine similarity), CKKS
homomorphic encryption at 128-bit security \cite{cheon2017ckks}, and
differential privacy with Gaussian noise at
$\varepsilon \in \{1, 5, 10\}$.

\subsubsection{End-to-End RAG Evaluation}
For each retrieval method, we construct a complete RAG pipeline and measure
accuracy (fraction of correct answers) and Recall@$k$. Because our protocol
computes exact integer inner products, retrieved documents are identical to
plaintext, so the LLM receives identical context.

\subsubsection{Hardware and Software}
All experiments run on a single workstation with an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU,
64\,GB RAM, Python 3.12. The protocol uses NumPy with native uint64
arithmetic. Timing uses \texttt{time.perf\_counter\_ns()} with median over
at least 50 repetitions.


\subsection{Retrieval Accuracy}
\label{sec:exp_accuracy}

\Cref{tab:accuracy} presents retrieval ranking agreement on PubMedQA.
Our protocol achieves 100\% Recall@1 and Recall@5 agreement with
$\rho = 1.0000$ across both models and both privacy modes. The maximum
absolute similarity error is $3.3 \times 10^{-5}$.

DP produces functionally random retrieval at all tested budgets: even at
$\varepsilon = 10$, Recall@1 is below 0.6\% and Recall@5 is 0.0\%, with
the gold-standard document displaced by 325 to 495 rank positions on
average. The noise standard deviation exceeds the embedding signal by a
factor of 20 at $\varepsilon = 10$.
This pattern generalizes: on MedMCQA and MedQA, MPC achieves 99.7 to
100\% Recall@k across all configurations while DP remains below 0.2\%.

CKKS also preserves ranking accuracy (R@1 = 1.000 at $N \leq 500$),
confirming that MPC's advantage over CKKS is computational cost and
security level, not retrieval correctness (see \Cref{sec:exp_latency}).
Encoding precision degrades gracefully: both models reach near-perfect
agreement at $k \geq 12$, and the default $k = 16$ provides four bits
of margin.

\subsection{Latency and Throughput}
\label{sec:exp_latency}

Latency scales linearly with both corpus size $N$ and embedding dimension
$d$, as expected from the matrix-vector inner product structure of the
protocol. \Cref{tab:latency} reports median per-query latency at selected
corpus sizes.

In query-private mode at $d = 384$ (MiniLM), latency is sub-millisecond
for corpora up to $N = 250$ and remains under 2\,ms at $N = 1{,}000$,
yielding a throughput of 698 queries per second. In mutual-privacy mode,
the additional cost of the query mask exchange adds a constant factor of
approximately 2 to 3$\times$, with throughput of 283 queries per second
at $N = 1{,}000$. At $d = 768$ (MedCPT), latency roughly doubles

\subsection{Communication and Storage Overhead}
\label{sec:exp_communication}

Per-query communication scales linearly with $N$. At $N = 1{,}000$,
$d = 384$, query-private mode requires 21.6\,KB and mutual-privacy
27.6\,KB (the difference is the masked query vector $F$).
Per-document storage is $2d \times 8$ bytes per server: 6.0\,KB at
$d = 384$ versus 326\,KB for CKKS (54$\times$ overhead). A 10{,}000-document
corpus requires 59\,MB total versus 3.2\,GB for CKKS.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Deployment Considerations}

Practical deployment requires provisioning the two servers under separate
administrative domains, each covered by a HIPAA Business Associate Agreement.
The offline phase (document encoding, Beaver triple generation, and mask
precomputation) runs once per corpus update and takes seconds for corpora
under 10{,}000 documents. Beaver triples should be refreshed periodically
(e.g., weekly) to limit the impact of any single compromised random seed.
The online query latency of 1 to 4\,ms fits comfortably within the
multi-second response budgets typical of clinical decision support workflows.
Integration with existing EHR systems requires only an embedding endpoint
and a FHIR-compatible document retrieval interface at the client.


\subsection{Limitations}

Our approach has several limitations.
(1)~The security guarantee requires non-collusion between the two servers,
a trust assumption enforceable through BAAs but absent in single-server HE
approaches.
(2)~Fixed-point encoding introduces quantization error bounded by
$2^{-(k+1)}$ per coordinate; at $k=16$ this is $< 10^{-5}$ and does not
affect rankings in our experiments.
(3)~The protocol performs a linear scan over all $N$ documents; this is
practical at clinical corpus sizes (sub-millisecond at $N \leq 1{,}000$)
but composing with private approximate nearest neighbor pre-filtering is
needed for full-catalog retrieval ($N > 10^6$).
(4)~We assume honest-but-curious servers; extending to malicious adversaries
via SPDZ-style MACs \cite{damgard2012spdz} is straightforward but adds overhead.
(5)~The embedding model runs on the client; model privacy is out of scope.


\subsection{Ethical Considerations}

This work aims to enable beneficial healthcare AI applications while
strengthening patient privacy. We do not use real patient data in our
experiments; all evaluations use publicly available medical QA datasets.
The system is designed to protect patient privacy, not to circumvent
legitimate data access controls.


%% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%% ============================================================

We presented a two-server additive secret sharing protocol for
privacy-preserving healthcare RAG that achieves exact retrieval with
information-theoretic security. Evaluation on three medical QA benchmarks
confirms identical retrieval and LLM answer quality compared to plaintext,
while DP baselines degrade accuracy by 52 to 58\% and CKKS operates at
over 9{,}000$\times$ the computational cost. The protocol runs at
sub-millisecond latency with security guarantees that map directly to HIPAA
technical safeguards, including the Minimum Necessary standard. The
two-server deployment aligns with existing healthcare IT infrastructure,
and the non-collusion assumption is enforceable through BAAs with concrete
civil and criminal penalties.
Future work includes private approximate nearest neighbor search for
full-catalog retrieval, evaluation on real clinical data under IRB
approval, and hardening against malicious servers via authenticated
secret sharing.
